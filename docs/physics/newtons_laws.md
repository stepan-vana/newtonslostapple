Isaac Newton's three laws of motion form the bedrock of classical mechanics. These empirical laws describe the precise relationship between the forces acting upon a physical body and the resulting motion of that body.

---

## Newton's First Law of Motion

Newton's First Law states that a body will remain at rest or continue to move at a constant velocity in a straight line unless acted upon by a net external force. This property of matter to resist changes to its state of motion is known as **inertia**.



In practical terrestrial environments, isolating this law can be difficult due to omnipresent dissipative forces. For example, if a ball is rolled across a wooden board, it will eventually decelerate to a complete stop. This cessation of motion is not a failure of the first law, but rather the direct result of external forces—specifically dry friction from the board and aerodynamic drag from the surrounding air—acting against the velocity vector of the ball. In a vacuum free of gravitational and frictional fields, the ball would continue moving indefinitely at a constant speed.

---

## Newton's Second Law of Motion

Newton's Second Law quantifies the effect of a net external force on a body's motion. It states that the acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting upon it and inversely proportional to its mass. Essentially, any change in a body's velocity vector (acceleration) requires the application of a net force.



### Mathematical Formulation

The law is classically expressed as the product of the body's inertial mass and its acceleration vector:

$$\boxed{\vec{F} = m\vec{a}}$$

* **$\vec{F}$** — The net external force vector acting on the object ($\text{N}$).
* **$m$** — The inertial mass of the object ($\text{kg}$).
* **$\vec{a}$** — The resulting acceleration vector ($\text{m/s}^2$).

Alternatively, in its most fundamental calculus-based form, Newton defined this law as the time rate of change of linear momentum ($\vec{p}$):

$$\vec{F} = \frac{\mathrm{d}\vec{p}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\mathrm{d}(m\vec{v})}{\mathrm{d}t}$$

---

## Newton's Third Law of Motion 

Newton's Third Law dictates that forces always occur in matched pairs. It states that when one body exerts a force on a second body, the second body simultaneously exerts a force equal in magnitude and opposite in direction on the first body. 

### Empirical Demonstration

This principle can be illustrated through a simple mechanical experiment involving two individuals standing stationary on skateboards:

* **The Interaction:** If Skateboarder A physically pushes Skateboarder B, Skateboarder A is applying an outward force ($\vec{F}_{\text{action}}$) onto Skateboarder B.
* **The Result:** Concurrently, Skateboarder B's body exerts an equal and opposite force ($-\vec{F}_{\text{reaction}}$) back onto Skateboarder A. As a result, both individuals will immediately begin accelerating away from each other in opposite directions.
