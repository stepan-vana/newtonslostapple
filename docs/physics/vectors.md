In classical mechanics and mathematics, measurable physical quantities are classified into two foundational categories based on the dimensionality of the information required to define them: **scalars** and **vectors**.

---

## Scalars vs. Vectors

* **Scalar Quantities (Scalars):** These require only a single piece of information for a complete description—a numerical value (magnitude) and its corresponding physical unit. Examples include mass ($m$), time ($t$), and temperature ($T$). Stating that an object has a mass of $5 \, \text{kg}$ provides complete information.
* **Vector Quantities (Vectors):** These cannot be fully described by a single value. In addition to the numerical value (**magnitude**), a vector strictly requires a defined **direction** in space. Examples include velocity ($\vec{v}$), force ($\vec{F}$), and acceleration ($\vec{a}$).

### Analytical Example
Consider a moving automobile. The statement that the vehicle is traveling at $90 \, \text{km/h}$ describes only the *scalar speed* of the car. To accurately model its kinematics and predict its future position, the *direction* is indispensable—whether it is moving northbound, southbound, or deflecting at a $45^\circ$ angle. Velocity is therefore a vector quantity.

---

## Geometric Interpretation and Notation

In a geometric context, a vector is represented as a **directed line segment (an arrow)**.



This graphical arrow simultaneously conveys both critical pieces of information:
1. **Length of the Arrow:** Corresponds to the absolute value (magnitude) of the physical quantity. A longer arrow denotes a greater force or a higher speed.
2. **Orientation of the Arrow:** Indicates the exact direction in which the quantity acts through space.

### Mathematical Notation

In formal technical documentation, vectors are denoted by a letter topped with a right-facing arrow. This notation immediately signals that the quantity must be manipulated using the rules of vector algebra (e.g., vector addition).

The standard notation for a velocity vector is expressed as:

$$\boxed{\vec{v}}$$

To isolate and reference exclusively the **magnitude** of the vector (e.g., the speed of the vehicle regardless of its direction), the quantity is written either as a standard italicized character without the arrow, or enclosed within vertical bars representing the absolute value:

$$v \quad \text{or} \quad |\vec{v}|$$
